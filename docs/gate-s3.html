Multiplicity, in the noise yardstick. D1's paired SE is 1.2 s; its +11.6 s sits 9.7 SE from zero and 8 SE above the 2 s floor. Under a global null of no gain anywhere, 22 one-sided 2.5% tests give 0.55 expected false "lo > 0" wins; the chance that a null lane clears the floor by 8 SE is nil at any N this campaign will reach. The S2 gate's concern (two floor-edge promotions in a row, F10) does not apply to this step: under the stricter reading of the floor (whole interval above 2 s) D1 still wins. What the stricter reading changes is the champion D1 was judged against: under it M1, not W1-A, was champion, and a direct D1 against M1 paired interval is not in the ledger (point estimates chain to +17.3 s). If you take the stricter reading, I run D1 against M1 as a declared k = 1 lane (5 min) before any upload.

The rule, restated

A challenger wins when the paired bootstrap interval of its RMSE gain sits above zero and the point estimate clears the analytic optimism bound (E[max of k N(0,1)] × paired SE, k = the declared cell count) plus the 2 s floor. The interval crossing zero is a tie; a tie promotes the simpler model. Lane A decides promotion; lane B is judged on its own rows. No month feature (R7). Every lane is declared before it runs; an undeclared run is a diagnostic.

This verdict is only as good as this rule; attack the rule, not just the numbers.

Two places to attack it here. First, the rule judges one holdout (Jan + Jul 2025); D1's gain comes from plan-gap columns whose 2026 distribution is unknown (F12 below). Second, the rule promotes on RMSE over all rows; D1's gain is 53 s on the tail rows and 1.3 s on the body rows, so it is a tail model's win, and the tail is where 2026 transfer is least known (F2).
